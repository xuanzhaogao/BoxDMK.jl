Periodic potentials

bdmk_periodic computes the periodic Coulomb potential of a density $f$ that is periodic on the cell $\{A t : t \in [0,1)^3\}$ (lattice vectors the columns of $A$, any triclinic cell):

\[u(x) = \int_{\text{cell}} \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^3} \frac{f(y)}{|x - y - A n|}\,dy \qquad (k = 0 \text{ mode dropped}).\]

A density with nonzero mean is taken with a uniform neutralizing background (the $k = 0$ mode of the whole potential is dropped); for a neutral density this is the usual periodic potential.

Usage

A = [1.0 0.35 0.15; 0 1 -0.2; 0 0 1]       # or a tuple (L₁, L₂, L₃), or a number for a cube
res = bdmk_periodic(f, A; eps = 1e-6)

evaluate_periodic(res, [0.3, 0.7, 0.9])    # anywhere (wrapped into the cell)
res.pot                                    # (n, n, n, nboxes) on each box's Legendre grid

f(x) must be defined (periodically) slightly outside the cell. The forest lives in the rotated frame $x_\text{rot} = Q^T x$ of $A = QU$ ($U$ upper triangular); res.ft is the forest, res.istarget marks the boxes whose potential is computed (those meeting the cell) and res.grid holds the geometry.

Options (see the docstring for all): norder = 16; rc_factor = 0.25 (the level-0 boxes have side $r_c = d_\min / \lceil 1/\text{rc\_factor} \rceil$, $d_\min$ the smallest distance between lattice planes); targets (3 × N points: only the leaves holding them are computed, values in res.target_pot); f_batch; timings.

Repeated solves

A plan keeps the forest, the tables and the buffers; each solve resamples the new density on the plan's forest:

plan = periodic_plan(f0, A; eps = 1e-6)    # forest adapted to f0
for step in 1:nsteps
    res = periodic_solve!(plan, f_step)
end

Work-saving rules

All are on by default and change the result by less than eps:

  • Halo copies, core interpolation. When the level-0 cubes tile the cell, the subtrees of the image halo are copies of their lattice images and only the central forest samples $f$. Otherwise only the boxes that meet the cell sample $f$, and the others interpolate the leaf polynomials at the periodic images of their nodes (interpolate = false gives the forest of the C++ builder).
  • Negligible sources (skip_negligible). Boxes whose density cannot change the potential by eps relative are left out of the real-space part.
  • Single-precision near field (near32, default for eps ≥ 1e-6). The dense same-level near field runs in Float32.

Sampling the density once

With sample_once = true (the default), $f$ is evaluated only by the adaptive refinement of the boxes that meet the cell. That is the one stage that needs $f$, to decide the forest. Everything after it works on the polynomials, which resolve $f$ to eps:

  • the boxes added for the 2:1 balance take their parent's polynomial;
  • the boxes outside the cell copy their lattice images or interpolate the core leaves (see above);
  • the wide source tree of non-tiled cells interpolates the forest leaves instead of sampling $f$ again. Its nodes are an affine image of a tensor grid, upper triangular in the rotated frame, so on each leaf the polynomial is contracted along z, then y, then x (about 90 ns per node);
  • a plan re-solve evaluates $f$ on the leaves of the adaptive refinement only and derives the other boxes in the same way.

timings[1] is the sampling stage; the rest of the time does not depend on the cost of $f$. Compared with sampling $f$ everywhere (sample_once = false, the C++ rules), $f$ is called 2–4 times less often, the potential changes by less than eps (at most 0.4 eps in the tests), and the accuracy against Ewald is unchanged.

Fast densities

At high accuracy, evaluating a point-by-point density can take half of the single-thread time. A batch density f_batch!(values, points) (values (1, npts), points (3, npts)) evaluates a whole box at once and can use SIMD. For example, density_batch! in benchmark/periodic_vs_cpp.jl evaluates a periodized Gaussian mixture on 8 points at a time with SIMD.jl and a vectorized exp. It is about 30 times faster per point than the scalar version. With sample_once it shortens only the sampling stage, for example 0.67 → 0.10 s on the hexagonal cell at eps = 1e-9 (see Performance):

res = bdmk_periodic(nothing, A; eps = 1e-9, f_batch = (v, p) -> density_batch!(v, p, mixture))

Method

The implementation in src/native/periodic/ follows the C++ bdmk_laplace3d_pot_periodic.

  • Forest. In the rotated frame, a grid of cubes of side $r_c$ covers the cell plus a one-box image halo. Each cube is refined adaptively, and the forest is 2:1 balanced.
  • Kernel split. $1/r$ is written as a sum of Gaussians (the u-series). The Gaussians that decay within $r_c$ go through the free-space box machinery on the forest (halo boxes act as sources only).
  • Wide Gaussians are summed in reciprocal space, exactly per axis with $e^{-i\alpha\xi} = \sum_l (2l+1)(-i)^l j_l(\alpha) P_l(\xi)$.
    • Source: Legendre moments of one period. They come from the level-0 proxy charges when the cubes tile the cell, and otherwise from an adaptive tree in fractional coordinates, where $e^{-2\pi i m\cdot t}$ is separable. That tree also refines wherever the main forest is much finer, so narrow features cannot hide between its sample points.
    • Target: the Legendre coefficients of the wide potential on each level-0 cube. This is a separable product for orthorhombic cells. Otherwise $k = 2\pi U^{-T} m$ is lower triangular, and the contraction runs slice by slice. The coefficients are injected into the level-0 proxy potentials before the downward pass.
    • The density is real, so only half of the modes are computed. No FFT is needed.

Accuracy

Against an Ewald-summed Gaussian mixture, the max error is at most eps relative to $\max|u|$ (0.3–1.0 eps over 3000 nodes per case), and the relative $L^2$ error is 1–4 eps, at eps = 1e-3, 1e-6, 1e-9. This holds on cubic, orthorhombic (1:1.5:2, 1:√2:√3), sheared, hexagonal and elongated (1:1:4) triclinic cells, for neutral and charged densities (test/test_periodic.jl). A lone spike of width $1.4\cdot10^{-3}$ on a sheared cell, a known failure of the C++ wide source (error $1.9\cdot10^{-5}$), is computed to $6.5\cdot10^{-10}$ at eps = 1e-9. On the C++ benchmark problems both codes build the same forest, and the potentials agree to $2\cdot10^{-13}$ at eps = 1e-9.
