Quasi-2D (slab) potentials

bdmk_q2d computes the Coulomb potential of a density in a slab that is periodic in x and y (in-plane lattice $A = [a_1\ a_2]$) and free in z:

\[u(x) = \int \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^2} \frac{f(y)}{|x - y - (An, 0)|}\,dy .\]

The density must vanish outside the slab $z_0 \le z \le z_1$. A neutral density is the default (checked). With charged = true a net charge $Q$ per cell is allowed, and the potential is fixed by the gauge $u + 2\pi Q|z|/|A| \to 0$ far from the slab. The potential is computed inside the slab.

L = (100.0, 100.0)                       # rectangle; a number for a square
res = bdmk_q2d(f, L, (0.0, 1.0); eps = 1e-6)
evaluate_periodic(res, [12.3, 45.6, 0.5])

A = [10.0 5.0; 0.0 5sqrt(3)]             # oblique (here hexagonal) cell, columns a₁, a₂
res = bdmk_q2d(f, A, (-7.0, 7.0); eps = 1e-6, charged = true)

plan = q2d_plan(f0, L, (0.0, 1.0); eps = 1e-6)   # repeated solves
res = periodic_solve!(plan, f1)

Options are those of bdmk_periodic, plus:

  • rc, the side of the level-0 boxes, with $r_c \le \min(d, H)$. Here $d$ is the in-plane spacing of the lattice lines ($\min(L_1, L_2)$ for a rectangle) and $H = z_1 - z_0$.
  • The default rc: for a rectangle, the largest value dividing both sides, so that the cubes tile the cell ($r_c = H$ for a thin slab); otherwise $\min(d, H)$.

The forest and res.pot are in the frame rotated so that $a_1$ lies along x.

Densities given by values, one-period integrals

For densities known only through a basis, as in ISDF:

  • P = sample_points(plan) gives the points where a solve samples the density.
  • periodic_solve!(plan, v) takes the values v[j] at those points.

period_quadrature gives a quadrature over exactly one period, with nodes Q.x and weights Q.w. It is built on parallelepiped cells in fractional coordinates and refined to the forest's resolution. period_values!(out, Q, res) interpolates the potential to its nodes. For example, $\int_{\text{cell}} g\,u$ is sum(Q.w .* gq .* out).

Method

The narrow Gaussians of the u-series (those that decay within rc) go through the real-space box machinery, as in the periodic solver, with image boxes in x and y only. The wide Gaussians are summed after Poisson summation over the in-plane lattice:

\[\sum_n e^{-|x - y - n\circ L|^2/\delta} = \frac{\pi\delta}{L_1 L_2} \sum_{\dot k} e^{-\delta|\dot k|^2/4} e^{i\dot k\cdot(\dot r - \dot r')} \, e^{-(z - z')^2/\delta}, \qquad \dot k = 2\pi(m_1/L_1, m_2/L_2).\]

  • In the plane, the transforms between Legendre moments of the source cells and the modes $\dot k = 2\pi A^{-T} m$ are exact per-axis Legendre–spherical-Bessel expansions (half space $m_1 \ge 0$), as in the periodic solver. The source cells tile one period: the level-0 cubes when they tile a rectangle, else the cells of a tree in fractional coordinates. They are factored through the uniform grid of cells: with $c_s = (s+\tfrac12) r_c$ and $k = 2\pi m/(m_t r_c)$, $e^{-ikc_s} = e^{-i\pi m/m_t} e^{-2\pi i (m \bmod m_t) s/m_t}$. The sum over cells is then a (real) FFT of length $m_t$ per Legendre degree, and the modes fold onto its frequencies. This is exact, with no window or oversampling as in a NUFFT, and costs O(N log N) instead of O(modes × cells) per axis. For an oblique cell the cubes do not tile the period, and two back-transforms (modes to cube coefficients) are available.
    • Dense: the triangular transform of the periodic solver ($k_2$ depends on $m_1$). It is the exact projection of each plane wave, done with GEMMs, at a cost cubic in the number of cubes per axis.
    • NUFFT: a type-2 NUFFT evaluates each z component of the potential, a real 2D Fourier series in $\xi = 2\pi A^{-1}x$, at $q = p_e + 8$ Gauss nodes per axis of each target level-0 cube.
      • Kernel: exponential of semicircle, fine grid twice the band, tolerance 0.01 eps.
      • The values are projected onto the cube's Legendre basis (degree $< p_e$), the same coefficients the dense path injects into the proxy potentials, so the downward pass and level-0 leaves are unchanged.
      • The number of targets is proportional to the number of cubes, and the cost is about linear in it.
    • The option wide_nufft (true, false, or nothing = automatic, by a calibrated cost model) chooses between them. The NUFFT pays off from about 50 cubes per axis: 4× faster at 192 × 111 cubes and eps = 1e-6, with 0.6× the memory. The two agree to about 0.1 eps (benchmark/q2d_nufft.jl, benchmark/results/q2d_nufft/).
  • In z, there is no Fourier transform and no zero padding. On a level-0 z-cell (side rc) every wide Gaussian is smooth, so its z-convolution is a small exact matrix in the Legendre basis of the cells, computed once by quadrature. For each mode the z-part is $\sum_\ell c_\ell(\dot k) G_\ell$, applied to all modes by one product per Gaussian. This is the idea of the Fourier–Chebyshev solver of the spectral SOG method for quasi-2D systems (Gao, Jiang, Liang, Xu and Zhou), made exact in the cell basis.
  • The mode $\dot k = 0$ takes the kernel of all wide Gaussians, $D(z) = \sum_\ell (\pi w_\ell \delta_\ell/L_1L_2)(e^{-z^2/\delta_\ell} - 1)$, which converges (the tail beyond the u-series is summed in closed form) and tends to the exact $-2\pi|z|/|A|$. The constants left out cancel for a neutral density. For a charged one they set the gauge: the narrow images add $Q\sum_{\rm narrow}\pi w\delta/|A|$. The u-series stops resolving $1/r$ below $2^{-16} r_c$ (where it saturates), which shifts the whole kernel by $1.3\times10^{-5}\,r_c/|A|$. Both constants are subtracted. It plays the role of the separate $k = 0$ term of 2-periodic Ewald summation (Lindbo and Tornberg).

Accuracy and cost

Against a 2-periodic Ewald sum (test/q2d_oracle.jl), the max error is 0.03–0.23 eps relative to $\max|u|$ at eps = 1e-3, 1e-6, 1e-9. The tests (test/test_q2d.jl) cover:

  • a square cell, a 2 × 3 cell and a thin 4 × 4 × 0.5 slab;
  • an oblique cell and a 1 × √2 rectangle (cubes not tiling);
  • charged slabs, rectangular and oblique, with rel. L2 error 3e-10 at eps = 1e-9.

For the 100 × 100 × 1 slab at eps = 1e-6 (rc = 1, 389k modes), the wide part takes 133 ms of 0.84 s at 48 threads. See Performance.
